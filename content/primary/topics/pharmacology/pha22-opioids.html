For long term opioid use → dose needs to be increased intermittently due to tolerance

Opioids

  • Endogenous opioid peptides → endorphins (from hypothalamus/pituitary, bind mu receptor & delta) → analgesia & euphoria
  • Enkephalins → delta & mu receptors → nociception
  • Dynorphins → kappa receptor → analgesia, appetite, circadian control

Gi/o receptors → inhibit adenylyl cyclase → ↓cAMP → ↓voltage-gated Ca2+ influx & ↑K+ efflux = hyperpolarisation

  • Mu receptor (M1=analgesia, M2=resp depression/GI/euphoria/HR effects) → morphine, buprenorphine, β-endorphin, enkephalins; antagonists: naloxone, naltrexone, nalorphine
  • Delta receptor → enkephalins; antagonist naltrindole; produces analgesia + ↑GI transit
  • Kappa receptor → morphine, pentazocine, dynorphins; antagonists naloxone/naltrexone; produces analgesia, sedation, miosis, dysphoria
  • Inflamed tissue → ↑opioid receptor recruitment, ↑opioid release from peripheral nerves

Central action → ↓afferent signalling in the substantia gelatinosa of the dorsal horn (at ↑dose = ↑muscle rigidity)

  • Pre-synaptic activation of opioid receptors ⇒ ↓substance P, ↓glutamate release
  • Post-synaptic activation ⇒ hyperpolarisation
  • ↑activity of descending pathways from the periaqueductal grey (PAG) via the nucleus raphe magnus in the medulla → ↑NA/5-HT release
  • PAG GABAergic interneurons normally inhibit descending pathways → opioids inhibit this inhibition = disinhibition

Classification

  • Natural = morphine, codeine, thebaine
  • Semi-synthetic = oxycodone, buprenorphine
  • Synthetic = remifentanil, fentanyl, methadone
  • Relative potency: remifentanil/fentanyl ~100, alfentanil ~20, morphine 1 (sufentanil ~1000)
  • Rapidly metabolised by esterases; ↑Vd; fast onset relates to ↑lipid solubility and the proportion ionised/dose required

Diamorphine

↑lipid solubility vs morphine ∴ used intrathecally/spinally

  • Fentanyl has even greater lipid solubility ∴ used in transdermal patches
  • Morphine metabolism → morphine-6-glucuronide (active, more potent analgesic) or morphine-3-glucuronide (no analgesic activity; neuroexcitatory) → renal excretion
  • Side effects → semi (chest wall rigidity) + class effects (↓ACTH, prolactin & GH) + well-known opioid side effects; itch mediated via histamine release with morphine & pethidine
  • Pethidine → mu agonist but structurally different to morphine, readily crosses the placenta, interacts with MAOIs, epileptogenic, lower potency than morphine
  • Fentanyl/alfentanil vs remifentanil = remifentanil is an ester (metabolised via esterases)
  • Tramadol - has two mechanisms: (1) weak agonist of opioid receptors & inhibitor of 5-HT reuptake (2) inhibits NA reuptake in the descending pathway - only partially reversed by naloxone; a weak opioid can achieve Emax at a high dose but a partial agonist cannot; associated with less respiratory depression
  • Codeine → metabolised via CYP2D6 O-demethylation → morphine (~10%); glucuronidated → codeine-6-glucuronide (~80%); N-demethylated via CYP3A4 → norcodeine (~10%); some people lack this enzyme ∴ variable analgesic effect; bioavailability ~50%
  • Dihydrocodeine = semi-synthetic analogue, bioavailability ~20%, more potent but less lipophilic than codeine
  • Buprenorphine acts on all opioid receptors → ↑affinity for mu, slow dissociation ∴ effect can last up to 24hr; available transdermal, intranasal, sublingual routes; small, potent, lipophilic molecule
  • Naloxone → mu opioid receptor antagonist, 30-60min duration → dose-dependent reversal → side effects appear before analgesia is lost at low doses
  • Naltrexone → mu & kappa opioid receptor antagonist, oral bioavailability possible, t½ ~4hr, longer lasting action than naloxone
