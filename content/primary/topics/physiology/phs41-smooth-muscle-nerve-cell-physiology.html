Smooth muscle - 2 types

(1) Visceral - syncytial function, AP propagates via gap junctions

(2) Multi-unit - fine control, no syncytial function, no spontaneous AP, each fibre controls one cell

Contraction sequence

(1) Initiation - involuntary, energy from glycolysis, under ANS control; ↑Ca2+ enters from the ECF, not the SR

(2) Ca2+ binds calmodulin → activates calmodulin-dependent myosin light chain kinase → phosphorylates the myosin head → ATPase activity → cross-bridge formation

(3) Speed of contraction: slower onset & longer duration than skeletal muscle due to lack of T-tubules and limited SR

(4) Relaxation: the Ca2+-calmodulin complex dissociates via a Ca2+-ATPase pump out of the cytoplasm → myosin is dephosphorylated by myosin light chain phosphatase; the cross-bridge can remain intact = 'latch-bridge' mechanism → sustained contraction with ↓energy cost

Plasticity

Initial ↑tension with stretch then falls - unlike skeletal muscle this doesn't correlate directly with length, so compliance can adjust (e.g. the bladder).

Unstable resting membrane potential (~-50mV); spontaneous activity from oscillators in the membrane potential; some pacemaker cells have flexible locations.

Neural control

PNS - ACh → M3 receptor → phospholipase C + IP3 → ↑Ca2+ influx → ↑motility, secretion, sphincter relaxation. SNS - α receptor → IP3 + diacylglycerol → phospholipase C; β receptor → cAMP → opposite effects.

NO relaxes smooth muscle: ACh/stretch → ↑Ca2+ in the endothelial cell → NO synthase → NO → guanylate cyclase → cGMP → protein kinase G → ↓intracellular Ca2+ → relaxation.

Nerve cell physiology

  • Gibbs-Donnan effect: [K+ + Cl- + intracellular anions] inside > [K+ + Cl-] outside
  • Axonal propagation: orthodromic = in the normal physiological direction; antidromic = in the opposite direction to normal (an axon can conduct either way if stimulated mid-axon)
  • Neurone types: monopolar = one axon (e.g. ANS); bipolar = 2 axons (e.g. retina/olfactory); multipolar = 3+ dendrites and 1 axon (e.g. CNS)
  • Neurotransmitters synthesised in the ER of the cell body, travel via axoplasmic flow: anterograde = rapid via microtubules; retrograde = transport of old vesicles back to the cell body
  • Saltatory conduction: propagation via Na+/K+ channels concentrated at the nodes of Ranvier

Glia

  • Astrocytes: maintain electrolyte balance (especially K+), support neurones, fibrous type forms the blood-brain barrier, absorb neurotransmitters, release NGF
  • Oligodendrocytes: myelinate CNS axons
  • Microglia: scavenger cells, derived from monocytes or resident, phagocytose debris

Outside the blood-brain barrier

'circumventricular organs' (like leaky pockets that communicate through hormones): posterior pituitary, median eminence of the hypothalamus, area postrema, subfornical organ, organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis.
