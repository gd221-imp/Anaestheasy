Most drugs cross the placenta by simple diffusion, so Fick's law applies: transfer depends on the drug, the gradient and the membrane.

What affects transfer

  • Molecular weight: under about 500 Da crosses easily; over about 1,000 Da (e.g. heparin, insulin) barely crosses.
  • Lipid solubility: lipid-soluble drugs cross readily.
  • Ionisation: only the un-ionised fraction crosses. Quaternary ammonium drugs are permanently charged and cross very little.
  • Ion trapping: fetal pH is slightly lower than maternal. Weak bases (local anaesthetics, opioids) become more ionised in the fetus and are trapped there, more so if the fetus is acidotic.
  • Protein binding: only free drug crosses; highly bound drugs such as bupivacaine cross less than lidocaine.
  • Gradient and placenta: maternal concentration, uteroplacental blood flow, surface area and thickness of the membrane, and placental metabolism.

Common anaesthetic drugs

  • Cross readily: induction agents (propofol, thiopental, ketamine), volatile agents, opioids, local anaesthetics, atropine, beta-blockers (e.g. labetalol: fetal bradycardia and hypoglycaemia), magnesium (neonatal hypotonia), warfarin.
  • Cross minimally: muscle relaxants including suxamethonium, glycopyrronium, neostigmine, heparin and LMWH, insulin.
  • Practical point: for surgery during pregnancy, neostigmine given with glycopyrronium can cause fetal bradycardia (some neostigmine crosses, glycopyrronium does not), so atropine is sometimes preferred.

From your notes

  • Suxamethonium: does not cross the blood-brain barrier or the placental barrier.
  • Pethidine: readily crosses the placenta.
  • Hydralazine: crosses the placenta.
  • Remifentanil PCA: crosses the placenta but is rapidly metabolised by fetal esterases.
  • Pregnancy pharmacokinetics: ↑cardiac output → ↑hepatic blood flow; ↑enterohepatic circulation; placental enzymes.
