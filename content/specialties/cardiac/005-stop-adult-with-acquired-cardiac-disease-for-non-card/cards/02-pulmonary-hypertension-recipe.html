Pre-op

  • A syndrome, not a diagnosis
  • Definition (2022 ESC/ERS): mean PAP above 20 mmHg at rest on right heart catheterisation; pre-capillary if PVR above 2 Wood units. A chronic rise leads to RVH and increased preload
  • One of the highest-risk groups for non-cardiac surgery
  • Pre-op questions: What is the cause? How severe? Is the patient clinically compensated? What is the procedural risk? Where should the procedure take place? Who else needs to be involved?

History (Hx)

  • Cause and group:
    • 1: PAH (pre-capillary): idiopathic, heritable, drug and toxin induced, connective tissue disease (e.g. Sjögren's), HIV, schistosomiasis. Most concerning: perioperative mortality doubled (2×) for non-cardiac surgery; often young women
    • 2: left heart disease (most common)
    • 3: lung disease or hypoxia
    • 4: chronic thromboembolic (CTEPH): the only curable type, with pulmonary endarterectomy or lung transplantation; untreated mortality 90% at 3 years
    • 5: multifactorial
  • WHO functional class; exercise tolerance (6-minute walk distance)
  • Symptoms of RV failure: exertional syncope, chest pain, oedema
  • PH therapies: endothelin receptor antagonists, PDE5 inhibitors, riociguat, prostacyclins (IV, subcutaneous or inhaled), selexipag
  • Anticoagulants; home oxygen
  • Urgency and type of surgery; risk scores often do not fit pulmonary hypertension

Examination (O/E)

  • Airway
  • Signs of RV failure: raised JVP, hepatomegaly, peripheral oedema
  • Loud P2
  • Saturations and baseline oxygen requirement

Investigations (Ix)

  • ECG (RV strain, right axis deviation)
  • Echo: dimensions (RV size relative to the LV) and function (TAPSE), estimated PA pressure, pericardial effusion
  • Right heart catheter data (mPAP, PVR)
  • Inhaled NO provocation (acute vasoreactivity) test: done at right heart catheterisation in idiopathic, heritable or drug-induced PAH; inhaled NO 20–40 ppm. Positive = mPAP falls by ≥10 mmHg to ≤40 mmHg with an unchanged or increased cardiac output. Responders (around 10%) can be treated with high-dose calcium channel blockers; check the PH centre letter for the result
  • PFTs: isolated reduced DLCO (<60% predicted)
  • NT-proBNP or BNP
  • ABG, U&E, LFTs, coagulation
  • CXR; CPET or 6-minute walk test
  • PH centre letters

Optimise

  • Are there non-operative options? Can we modify our surgical or anaesthetic technique?
  • Discuss with the PH centre, especially if CTEPH or PAH; consider doing the surgery there, or at least at a cardiac centre
  • Continue all PH therapies; never interrupt an IV prostacyclin infusion (dedicated line)
  • Optimise fluid status and diuretics
  • Anticoagulation plan
  • Plan regional versus GA and the post-op level of care
  • High risk: consider ECMO back-up
  • Anaesthetic questions: preload dependent? Is the cardiac output fixed? What is the RV function like? What is the PVR? What will happen with increased intrathoracic pressure (can I adjust my ventilatory strategy)? What is the anticoagulation plan?

Intra-op

Equipment

  • Arterial line, CVC, ± PA catheter, TOE
  • Inhaled nitric oxide
  • Defibrillator pads

Induction

  • PVR down and SVR up
  • 20G cannula, then 16G once asleep
  • Arterial line before induction
  • Slow, titrated induction; keep SVR above PVR and avoid hypotension (RV coronary perfusion)
  • Vasopressor running before induction (noradrenaline; vasopressin raises SVR without raising PVR)
  • Avoid anything that raises PVR: hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis, hypothermia, pain, light anaesthesia at laryngoscopy
  • Neuraxial: slowly titrated epidural or CSE rather than a single-shot spinal; peripheral blocks where possible

Maintenance

  • High FiO2; normocapnia or mild hypocapnia
  • Low PEEP and moderate tidal volumes (avoid high intrathoracic pressure)
  • Maintain sinus rhythm
  • Avoid both fluid overload and hypovolaemia
  • Laparoscopy: low pressures and strict CO2 control
  • Pulmonary vasodilators: inhaled nitric oxide, inhaled iloprost
  • Inotropes for RV dysfunction: milrinone, dobutamine, adrenaline
  • Pulmonary hypertensive crisis (presents with hypotension): 100% oxygen, hyperventilate, correct acidosis, inhaled nitric oxide, IV vasodilators or inotrope, noradrenaline or vasopressin to keep SVR up, optimise CVP with diuretics or CVVH, head up, no more fluid; ECMO. Exclude and treat tamponade

Emergence

  • Smooth emergence: avoid coughing and straining
  • Full reversal
  • Good analgesia before waking

Post-op

Complications

  • RV failure and pulmonary hypertensive crisis
  • Arrhythmias
  • Hypoxia
  • Bleeding on anticoagulants
  • Sudden death

Surgical

Immediate

None specific.

Delayed

None specific.

Anaesthetic

Immediate

  • Pulmonary hypertensive crisis
  • RV failure with hypotension at induction

Delayed

  • RV failure
  • Arrhythmias

Analgesia

  • Regional and multimodal: manage pain well to prevent a rise in PVR
  • Avoid oversedation (hypercapnia raises PVR)

Destination

  • HDU or ICU for the first 48–72 hours
  • Continue PH therapies or restart early
  • Involve the PH team

Pulmonary hypertensive crisis / failed RV

  • Treat precipitating factors: correct hypoxia, acidosis and hypercarbia; treat sepsis, anaemia and thrombus
  • Optimise RV preload: diuretics vs fluids (usually not to be given fluids)
  • Reduce RV afterload: prostanoid (epoprostenol); inhaled nitric oxide
  • Maintain perfusion pressures: vasopressor – noradrenaline; add vasopressin if the noradrenaline dose climbs, as at higher doses noradrenaline's alpha-1 action in the lungs increases PVR
  • Improve RV contractility: inotropes, inodilators; maintain sinus rhythm
  • If all else fails, replace the RV: VA ECMO
