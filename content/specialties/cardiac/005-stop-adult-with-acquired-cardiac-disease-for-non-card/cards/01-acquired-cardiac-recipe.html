Pre-op

  • Pre-op assessment and planning: 2022 ESC Guidelines on cardiovascular assessment and management of patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery (Halvorsen S et al.) ↗

History (Hx)

  • Type of disease: IHD, CAD, heart failure, valve disease (especially AS), AF and other dysrhythmias, hypertension
  • IHD: angina (CCS class), MI and its date; stents (type, date, indication)
  • Functional capacity: METs (can they climb 2 flights of stairs?), DASI; NYHA class
  • Revised Cardiac Risk Index
  • Devices: pacemaker or ICD, pacing dependence, last check
  • Drugs: what medications are needed to control symptoms? Antiplatelets, anticoagulants, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors or ARBs, SGLT2 inhibitors, diuretics, statins
  • Urgency and risk of the surgery

Examination (O/E)

  • Airway
  • Signs of heart failure
  • Murmurs (ejection systolic murmur: think AS)
  • Heart rate and rhythm; BP
  • Vascular access

Investigations (Ix)

  • Tests are used to decide whether there is an intervention that can mitigate intra-op risk: is there a disease that needs treatment before non-cardiac surgery? (N.B. most interventions take a long time to have an effect)
  • ECG
  • FBC, U&E (renal function for DOAC timing), coagulation if anticoagulated
  • NT-proBNP or BNP and a baseline high-sensitivity troponin if 65 or over, or with known cardiovascular disease or risk factors, before intermediate- or high-risk surgery
  • If they can climb 2 flights of stairs (more than 4 METs), proceed unless they have new cardiac symptoms
  • Echocardiogram for a new murmur, unexplained breathlessness, poor functional capacity or a raised NT-proBNP before high-risk surgery:
    • What information do we want from it?
    • Limitations of the ejection fraction (e.g. it overestimates function in mitral regurgitation)
    • Are the chambers dilated? Dilatation signifies decompensation
  • CPET or stress imaging if functional capacity is poor and surgery is high risk; coronary angiography
  • Other risk scores: EuroSCORE, SORT, RCRI
  • Cardiology letters, device check

Optimise

  • Cardiology input for unstable symptoms, new findings or recent events
  • Stents: elective surgery ideally 6 months after elective PCI and 12 months after ACS. Time-sensitive surgery: at least 1 month of DAPT after elective PCI; at least 3 months after PCI for high-risk ACS
  • Antiplatelets: continue aspirin after PCI if bleeding risk allows (stop 7 days before high-bleeding-risk surgery, e.g. intracranial or spinal); if a P2Y12 inhibitor must stop: ticagrelor 3–5 days, clopidogrel 5 days, prasugrel 7 days before
  • Discussion point: which preventative medications to continue?
    • Beta-blockers: always continue
    • Statins: continue
    • Consider holding ACE inhibitors and ARBs on the day of surgery in patients without heart failure (prevents hypotension)
    • Stop SGLT2 inhibitors at least 3 days before intermediate- or high-risk surgery
  • DOAC stopping time by drug, renal function and bleeding risk (5 half-lives before neuraxial); bridging only for high thrombotic risk (e.g. mechanical valve)
  • Severe symptomatic AS before elective intermediate- or high-risk surgery: AVR (TAVI or surgical) is recommended first; consider it in asymptomatic severe AS
  • AF: rate control
  • Device reprogramming plan
  • Treatment escalation plan

Intra-op

  • Essential physiology
    • The supply: CPP = aortic diastolic BP − LVEDP. Defend the aortic diastolic pressure first (metaraminol can be given first) and protect against a rise in LVEDP (don't give fluid)
    • The demand: myocardial VO2 depends on heart rate, contractility, preload and wall tension (Laplace's law: T = Pr/2h)
    • The crux: minimise the effects you have on the patient, i.e. mitigate the effects of the anaesthetic

Equipment

  • Arterial line, ± CVC, ± cardiac output monitor or TOE
  • Five-lead ECG with ST analysis
  • Defibrillator pads

Induction

  • In general: metaraminol, limited fluid and a gentle induction
  • 20G cannula, then 16G once asleep
  • Arterial line before induction in significant disease
  • Cardiostable induction with an opioid to blunt the pressor response
  • Regional or neuraxial where antiplatelets and anticoagulants allow

Maintenance

  • "Keep the BP up": avoid prolonged MAP below 60–65 mmHg; use a vasopressor infusion
  • "Don't give unnecessary fluid": goal-directed fluids; in cardiac failure, resume afterload reduction with diuresis post-op
  • Maintain haemoglobin and temperature
  • ± cardiac output monitor
  • Treat AF promptly
  • Device plan; bipolar diathermy

Emergence

  • Avoid hypertension and tachycardia
  • Full reversal
  • Extubate awake
  • Arterial line into recovery

Post-op

Complications

  • Myocardial injury after non-cardiac surgery (MINS): troponin at 24 and 48 hours if high risk
  • MI
  • Heart failure
  • New AF
  • Stent thrombosis
  • Bleeding
  • AKI

Analgesia

  • Regional where possible
  • Paracetamol; avoid NSAIDs in heart failure, IHD or AKI risk
  • Careful opioid titration

Destination

  • Level 2 or 3 care if significant disease or major surgery
  • Restart antiplatelets within 48 hours (or once haemostasis allows) and anticoagulants as agreed
  • Device re-check
  • Cardiology follow-up for MINS or new AF

Lesion-specific goals

CPP = aortic diastolic BP − LVEDP

  • IHD and CAD: balance supply and demand; avoid tachycardia, hypotension and anaemia. Indications for pre-op coronary angiography: chest pain (also in renal transplant patients)
  • Aortic stenosis: maintain SVR and sinus rhythm. Functional status is the number one question to ask. LVH is a compensatory mechanism (Laplace's law), so we know it is failing if the LV increases in size and the wall becomes thinner
  • Mitral stenosis: avoid tachycardia
  • Mitral regurgitation and other regurgitant lesions: keep the rate up and afterload down. How has the diuretic dose progressed? Echoes may look the same, but only because of an increasing diuretic dose
  • Heart failure: avoid myocardial depression and fluid overload
