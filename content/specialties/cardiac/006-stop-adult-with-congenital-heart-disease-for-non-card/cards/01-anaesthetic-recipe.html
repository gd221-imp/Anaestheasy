Pre-op

  • N.B. More patients with CHD are living into adulthood, so adult congenital heart disease is becoming more common in adult practice.
  • ACHD pre-op evaluation for non-cardiac surgery (Tables 1–3, adapted from Halvorsen S et al.): ESC clinical consensus statement: anaesthetic management of adults with CHD undergoing non-cardiac surgery (Eur Heart J Valvular Struct Heart Dis, 2026) ↗

History (Hx)

  • Lesion: repaired or unrepaired, palliated (e.g. Fontan), shunts; previous operations and residual problems
  • Symptoms and functional capacity (METs, can they climb 2 flights of stairs?). NYHA classification:
    • I: no limitation of physical activity
    • II: slight limitation; comfortable at rest, ordinary activity causes symptoms
    • III: marked limitation; comfortable at rest, less than ordinary activity causes symptoms
    • IV: symptoms at rest; any activity increases discomfort
  • Recent cardiac events or interventions
  • Devices (pacemaker, ICD)
  • Medications: antiplatelets, anticoagulants, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, diuretics
  • Urgency and risk of the surgery itself

Examination (O/E)

  • Airway
  • Signs of heart failure (raised JVP, crackles, oedema)
  • Murmurs
  • Cyanosis, saturations (baseline in cyanotic congenital disease)
  • Previous scars and vascular access

Investigations (Ix)

  • ECG; FBC, U&E, BNP or NT-proBNP
  • Echocardiogram if new murmur, symptoms or no recent study
  • CPET or functional testing if capacity unclear
  • Recent cardiology letters, device check
  • Coagulation if anticoagulated

Optimise

  • Discuss with cardiology or the adult congenital heart disease team; consider a specialist centre for complex lesions
  • Continue beta-blockers and statins
  • Plan for antiplatelets and anticoagulants (bridging if mechanical valve)
  • Hold ACE inhibitors and ARBs on the morning of surgery
  • Endocarditis prophylaxis only if indicated (follow local guidelines)
  • Device reprogramming plan
  • Treatment escalation plan
  • Risk assessment and planning care (2026 consensus statement):
    • Procedure risk (Table 2): A, low (e.g. breast, dental, thyroid, eye, minor gynae or orthopaedic, superficial surgery); B, intermediate (e.g. carotid, EVAR, head and neck, cholecystectomy, major hip or spine, renal transplant, caesarean section); C, high (e.g. aortic or major vascular, pancreatic or liver surgery, oesophagectomy, pneumonectomy, perforated bowel, cystectomy)
    • Patient risk (Table 3): 1, low (small uncorrected defects needing no treatment, or corrected CHD with no symptoms, residua or medication); 2, intermediate (residual haemodynamic abnormality such as severe AV valve regurgitation, or arrhythmias); 3, high (uncorrected cyanotic CHD, pulmonary hypertension, complex CHD, ventricular dysfunction needing medication, life-threatening arrhythmias, listed for transplant)
    • Plan in advance: MDT agreement, lesion-specific haemodynamic goals, bleeding and anticoagulation plan, post-op level of care
    • High-risk patients first on the list (limits dehydration from fasting in preload-dependent circulations; expert staff available)
    • Endocarditis prophylaxis as per current ESC guidelines; prophylactic phlebotomy to a target haematocrit is not advised
    • Cardiac devices or high arrhythmia risk: review by an electrophysiologist with ACHD expertise

Intra-op

  • Key components (European Heart Journal, valvular and structural heart disease, 2026): ESC clinical consensus statement: anaesthetic management of adults with CHD undergoing non-cardiac surgery (Eur Heart J Valvular Struct Heart Dis, 2026) ↗

Equipment

  • Standard monitoring, arterial line, ± CVC, ± cardiac output monitor or TOE (major surgery)
  • Large-bore IV access for high-risk or redo procedures
  • Depth of anaesthesia (EEG) monitoring; consider cerebral oximetry (not validated with high haematocrit)
  • Defibrillator pads if arrhythmia risk or device deactivated
  • Air filters on IV lines for shunts

Induction

  • 20G cannula, then 16G once asleep
  • Arterial line before induction if significant disease
  • Slow, titrated, cardiostable induction; avoid tachycardia and hypotension
  • Record baseline vital signs before induction, even when urgent; in critical patients, arterial line before induction or before neuraxial needle placement
  • No single agent is preferred (propofol and sevoflurane most used): propofol lowers BP, HR and contractility; volatiles lower SVR and cause tachycardia
  • Cyanotic patients: ketamine with a GABA-A agonist (etomidate, midazolam or propofol) helps avoid increasing right-to-left shunting; ketamine also helps in severe ventricular dysfunction, obstructive lesions and severe pulmonary hypertension
  • Regional and neuraxial: low-dose, incremental techniques to limit hypotension; peripheral nerve blocks to minimise opioids; neuraxial suits patients in whom a fall in SVR is not a concern
  • Lesion-specific goals:
    • Aortic stenosis: maintain SVR and sinus rhythm
    • Mitral stenosis: avoid tachycardia
    • Regurgitant lesions: keep the heart rate up and afterload down
    • Shunts: avoid air bubbles and changes in SVR/PVR balance
    • Left-to-right shunt: avoid a rise in SVR or fall in PVR (light anaesthesia, pain, hypothermia, hypovolaemia, hypocarbia, hyperoxia, alkalosis)
    • Right-to-left shunt: avoid a fall in SVR or rise in PVR (hypoxia, hypercarbia, acidosis, hypothermia, pain)

Maintenance

  • Haemodynamic targets agreed beforehand
  • Vasopressor infusion (e.g. noradrenaline or metaraminol)
  • Avoid fluid overload
  • Normothermia
  • Cardiac output monitoring if major surgery
  • Magnet or reprogramming plan for devices; pacemaker-dependent patients may benefit from a higher paced rate
  • Bipolar diathermy where possible
  • Treat hypovolaemia and tachycardia aggressively in severe outflow obstruction, unrestrictive shunts, Fontan circulation and cyanosis
  • Ventilation: lung-protective, low PEEP; minimise effects on PVR (positive pressure is poorly tolerated in Fontan and RV dysfunction)
  • Laparoscopy: gradual insufflation to 8–12 mmHg, strict CO2 control, lower-limb compression; discuss the effects of pneumoperitoneum with the surgeon beforehand
  • EEG-based depth of anaesthesia monitoring to guide dosing

Emergence

  • Avoid hypertension and tachycardia
  • Full reversal
  • Extubate awake
  • Continue arterial monitoring into recovery

Specific conditions

  1. Fontan circulation:
    • Systemic and pulmonary circulations are in series: blood flows passively to the lungs
    • Complications from venous back-pressure: liver congestion and cirrhosis, pleural and pericardial effusions, restrictive lung disease, protein-losing enteropathy
    • Functional status is very important (some are very active)
    • Positive pressure ventilation is poorly tolerated: raised intrathoracic pressure reduces pulmonary blood flow
    • Aim for as short an operation as possible
  2. Systemic right ventricle after atrial switch for transposition (Mustard or Senning):
    • The RV works as the systemic ventricle and tends to fail over time
  3. Congenitally corrected TGA (ccTGA):
    • Born with the transposition "corrected", but the RV is still the systemic ventricle, with the same complications
  4. Shunts:
    • Simple or complex?
    • If presenting as an adult, ask why it has not been corrected: most are straightforward to close, and closure prevents Eisenmenger syndrome
  5. Eisenmenger syndrome:
    • Right-to-left flow through an unrepaired septal defect
    • Worse while Eisenmenger physiology is evolving; once established, the PVR is fixed
    • The riskiest part is emergence: suction everything deeply, then leave them alone and let them wake up slowly (like a neuro wake-up)
    • Pulmonary hypertensive crisis: 100% oxygen, lower the CO2, magnesium, inhaled nitric oxide; often already on an iloprost infusion; ECMO as back-up

Post-op

Complications

  • Myocardial ischaemia or MINS (check troponin if high risk)
  • Heart failure
  • Arrhythmias
  • Thromboembolism or bleeding (anticoagulation)
  • Paradoxical embolism with shunts
  • AKI

Analgesia

  • Regional techniques where suitable, considering anticoagulation
  • Paracetamol; avoid NSAIDs in heart failure or AKI risk
  • Careful opioid titration

Destination

  • Level 2 or 3 care if significant disease or major surgery
  • Restart anticoagulants and antiplatelets as agreed
  • Device re-check if reprogrammed
  • Cardiology follow-up
