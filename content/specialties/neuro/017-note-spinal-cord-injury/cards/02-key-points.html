Physiological changes in acute spinal cord injury (complete transection):

  • Sensory: complete loss below the level acutely -> persists, plus neuropathic pain chronically
  • Motor: spinal shock (flaccid paralysis + areflexia) acutely -> spastic paralysis + hyper-reflexia chronically (around 3 months)
  • Autonomic: DVT risk, neurogenic shock, loss of temperature control, bowel/bladder dysfunction acutely -> autonomic dysreflexia chronically (sitting the patient up is part of its treatment)
  • Respiratory: injury at or above T8 causes intercostal muscle paralysis, relying solely on diaphragmatic contraction for inspiration; at or above C3 causes diaphragm paralysis needing IPPV (expiratory/abdominal contraction is needed for effective expiration and cough, so this is also lost)
  • Cardiovascular: above T6 causes neurogenic shock, increased VTE risk, reduced venous return, and poor arteriolar/venous tone (around 90% DVT/10% PE risk); injuries above T1-T4 cause bradycardia from loss of the cardioaccelerator fibres (T1-T4)
  • GI: reduced sympathetic tone relative to the enteric nervous system causes reduced gastric emptying, gastric ulceration, constipation, reduced bladder/bowel control, priapism, gallstones
